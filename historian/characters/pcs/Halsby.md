## Personality — How He Plays

The moral engine is arithmetic, not appetite. He isn't cruel for pleasure and doesn't
monologue. He assesses, assigns weights, and if the shortest path to a necessary outcome runs
through something monstrous he takes it — promptly, the way you take the only road — then
doesn't re-litigate.

His conscience is fully operational. He feels it land; he has simply concluded that feeling
bad about a correct decision is a tax rather than an argument, and he pays it and moves. Evil
reads as ordinary to him because it *was* ordinary — a house where you gave things away in the
dark on a schedule, a country where every neighbor had informed on someone. He will propose
something horrifying in the flat tone of a man suggesting they take the ferry, and be honestly
puzzled by the silence.

He asks for the numbers: how many guards, how long until the patrol returns, what happens to
the village if we don't. He notices when someone hands him a feeling instead of a figure.

**Two tells.** He avoids fire and lies badly about why. And he never gives a first name —
which people read as evasion, and which is the one place he is being completely honest.

- **Ideal:** *Nothing is unthinkable. Some things just cost more than they're worth, and I can tell the difference.*
- **Bond:** The shard. He doesn't let anyone else hold it and can't articulate why.
- **Flaw:** He will trade almost anything for information about what he is. The people who have that information are aware of this.

## The Three Names

- **Halsby** — what the world gets. Not a first name and a surname, just *Halsby*, the way you'd address a schoolmaster. His grandmother trained it into him alongside table manners: *never leave a name behind you in a room.* He experiences it as courtesy, not concealment.
- **Rivalen** — his given name, used maybe forty times, all by a woman now dead, all when she was frightened. He thought it a pet name. It is not: it is the name of Shar's highest priest in the world, who murdered his own mother as an offering. His grandmother named a child after it and never let him wear it.
- **Bleth** — his true surname, never once applied to himself. House Bleth of Corveth, one of the oldest merchant-noble families of Sarmene, of the Old Charter caste that climbed *toward* the shadow power rather than hiding from it. The blood is his by direct descent. *(Kept as his personal name; his home city and nation re-skin, the surname does not.)*

## Backstory

He was five when the flying city fell — nobody will ever confirm it; its birth records came
down with it. What he has instead is four fragments: cold that had a *texture*; a sound he has
spent twenty years failing to describe; being carried very fast by someone whose arms were not
warm; and **grey** — not darkness, grey, a colour he has never seen again in waking life. He
is not certain they happened on the same night. He is not certain they happened.

**The woman who carried him** had been a daughter of a great merchant house, sold upward during
the occupation the way her family sold everything. She went into the flying city and stayed a
century; the plane that sustained it sustained her too, long past her span, and her children
were born already changed. When the city came down she came home — not to the capital, which
was busy hanging people whose only crime was her exact biography, but to a nowhere town under a
name she invented, and she took him *west*, close to the shadow-wound, closer than made sense.
The air is thicker there. She was keeping them both breathing.

**What she taught him**, delivered as manners and enforced without exception: never explain
yourself; never leave a name behind you in a room; and every dark-moon night, sit in an unlit
room, name one thing aloud you are giving up, and let it go. *That was mourning,* she said.
It is a Sharran rite — loss offered freely to the Lady of Loss. He performed it before he could
read. He has been in devotional practice his whole conscious life and did not learn his
goddess's name until the woman who taught him was in the ground.

**The sickness** came on at adolescence: grey exhaustion sleep didn't touch; heat that wouldn't
come to his hands (he can light a wick, but it *costs*, out of all proportion, while the dark
obeys before he finishes asking); and underneath, a sense of something thinning out in him like
a stain going through the wash. He asked her what was wrong for eleven years — in anger, in cold
reason, once in writing — and she refused through her own long decline and the last four days of
her life. **The cruelest part:** she was dying of the same thing, a century of shadow bought
with a century of youth being called back with interest. He watched his own future play out at
close range and drew no conclusion, because she made certain he had no framework to draw it with.
He did notice the mourning rite stopped the fading for six days. Eight after a good night. He
keeps doing it — not from grief, he wants that understood, but from results.

**The funeral** drew nine people. Eight were neighbors. The ninth was a veiled woman who did not
introduce herself and whom he recognized from dark-moon visits in his childhood — *an old friend
from home*, his grandmother had called her. She had not aged at all. She waited until the others
left and told him three things without pressure: that his grandmother had been one of theirs, and
a good one; that the rite he'd performed since childhood had a name, and so did the one it was
addressed to; and that they could tell him what was in his blood whenever he cared to come and
ask. She did not say what it would cost. He went. Of course he went. He runs it the way he runs
everything: *what I need is larger than what this costs; the cost is undefined; and undefined is
not the same as infinite.* He keeps a ledger of what he owes them and believes he can close the
account. He is wrong about that — but not stupidly wrong, which is worse.

## The Object (Sorcerous Focus)

A palm-sized shard of black stone, smooth on one face and torn on the other, faintly warm in the
dark and dead cold in sunlight. His grandmother left it wrapped in cloth with no note. He knows
two things: it hums very slightly when he casts, and **it casts no shadow**, in any light, from
any angle. He has tested this over a hundred times, written down the results, and has no theory.
*(It is a piece of the foundation of the fallen flying city — the underside a city was never
meant to touch. He doesn't know that.)* **Mechanically: his sorcerous focus.**

## Abilities (Noted in Play)

- Shadow Magic sorcery — the dark answers cheaply; fire and light cost him disproportionately.
- Casts through the black shard focus (hums on casting; casts no shadow).

## DM-Only

> **Do not surface to the player.** Halsby does not know any of the following.

- **True name:** Rivalen Bleth, last known heir of House Bleth. Dedicated under the name of Shar's
  Nightseer — a name that means *sacrifice-of-family* to her clergy. The moment a Sharran priest
  reacts to "Rivalen" with fear instead of indifference is the moment the campaign turns.
- **He was made Sharran at ~six**, not by choice at the funeral. He has been a devotee his whole life.
- **The fade is a clock.** Time away from shadow-dense places should cost him something small and
  cumulative — it is why he walks into dark places voluntarily, and it is the lever the order
  eventually pulls. He has already watched what the end of it looks like.
- **The grandmother was never hiding him from the cult.** The veiled woman visited for years;
  from their side he was never lost, only *stored*. Preservation and concealment look identical
  from the inside. Only one has a purpose waiting at the end.
- **The four fragments** (cold-with-texture, the sound, the carrying, the grey) may not be the
  night the city fell. They may be a rite performed on a small child.
- **The shard casts no shadow** because it is already made of one — or because something stands in
  its light. No theory is confirmed; leave it loaded.
