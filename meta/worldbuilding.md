# World Building Principles

> **Base setting: standard D&D.** These notes are additives — flavors layered onto an otherwise normal D&D world. Pirates exist in a world that also has inns, merchants, dungeons, and kingdoms. Dinosaurs live in specific regions, not everywhere. No single element should dominate the world's identity. A player in a generic port town should feel like they're in D&D; a player in the right region might also encounter sky pirates. The palette enriches — it doesn't replace the foundation.

## Tone

Rich, layered, and politically intricate. Epic mythic depth (LotR) combined with dark political complexity (GoT). Multiple distinct biomes each with strong genre flavor. Grit and cultural specificity in the Warhammer vein — not defined by technology level, but by distinct civilizations with real pressures and histories.

## Core Themes

- Political intrigue and power struggles
- Resource scarcity and economic control
- Ancient mystery — the world is older than anyone knows; ruins and relics predate understanding
- Small people facing a vast world — scale of wonder, not just threat

## Setting Pillars

- **Biome diversity:** All standard biomes exist (forests, mountains, plains, tundra, swamps, coasts). Layered on top: desert/western, open sea, sky, and dinosaur territories — each with its own pirate/frontier culture
- **Magic is rare:** ~3/10. Universally known to exist; having it is a gift, not a learnable skill. LotR-ish — presence is significant, not routine. Creates hard social stratification between those who have it and those who don't.
- **Cultural depth over tech level:** Civilizations differ by values, history, and environment — Warhammer-style specificity

## World Textures

- **Coffee economy:** Rare, fetches high prices, desired by everyone. Gnomes dominate the trade historically — most brokers, roasters, and caravan operators are gnomish — but the market isn't locked. Other players exist; gnomes just got there first and stayed organized.
- **Remnant technology:** A pre-history civilization left ruins and relics the present world cannot explain or replicate. Introduce sparingly — one mission or arc element at a time, never as backdrop noise. What it was, what it built, and what ended it are unknown and should stay unknown. Functional relics feel wrong, not evil — they don't behave like magic or craftsmanship. No faction controls or understands this. D&D atmosphere holds; this is just the shadow of something older underneath it.
- **Technological ceiling:** Standard D&D is the common tone. Steampunk is the maximum technological reach for any faction — never exceeded, rarely approached.
- **Optimism persists:** Wonder and warmth exist even in dark settings. Party bonds form through shared experience. Small people from small places are capable of facing a world much larger than them — and knowing it.

## Influences

### Major
- Lord of the Rings (depth, magic rarity, mythic weight)
- Game of Thrones (political plots, moral complexity)
- Western genre (desert frontier, scarcity, outlaws)
- Pirate genre (sea and sky variants, magical or steampunk ships)
- Elden Ring (shattered ancient orders, cryptic ruins of fallen civilizations, gods who broke the world)

### Minor
- Warhammer 40k (planets where history has forgotten mighty ages — technology as lost inheritance, not progress)
- Redwall (tight-knit communities of smallfolk, cozy-dark contrast, simple lives interrupted by genuine menace — not anthro animals, but the texture of beloved places under threat)
- Grandia (optimism as survival — wonder persists despite darkness; party bonds form through shared experience, not exposition; small people from small places facing a world much larger than them)
