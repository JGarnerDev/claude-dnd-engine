# World Building Principles

> **Base setting: standard D&D.** These notes are additives — flavors layered onto an otherwise normal D&D world. Pirates exist in a world that also has inns, merchants, dungeons, and kingdoms. Dinosaurs live in specific regions, not everywhere. No single element should dominate the world's identity. A player in a generic port town should feel like they're in D&D; a player in the right region might also encounter sky pirates. The palette enriches — it doesn't replace the foundation.

## Tone

Rich, layered, and politically intricate. Epic mythic depth (LotR) combined with dark political complexity (GoT). Multiple distinct biomes each with strong genre flavor. Grit and cultural specificity in the Warhammer vein — not defined by technology level, but by distinct civilizations with real pressures and histories.

## Core Themes

- Political intrigue and power struggles
- Resource scarcity and economic control
- Ancient mystery — the world is older than anyone knows; ruins and relics predate understanding
- Small people facing a vast world — scale of wonder, not just threat

## Setting Pillars

- **Biome diversity:** All standard biomes exist (forests, mountains, plains, tundra, swamps, coasts). Layered on top: desert/western, open sea, sky, and dinosaur territories — each with its own pirate/frontier culture
- **Magic is rare:** ~3/10. Universally known to exist, rare in practice. LotR-ish — presence is significant, not routine. Creates hard social stratification between those who have it and those who don't.
- **Cultural depth over tech level:** Civilizations differ by values, history, and environment — Warhammer-style specificity

## Gods & Religion

Gods are real and present — not historical figures or distant abstractions, but beings capable of manifesting in the world now. They rarely engage in worldly affairs directly; when they do, it matters.

**Religion as a live mechanic:** Founding cults, building faiths, recruiting followers, and writing doctrine are accessible to players. NPCs can do the same — religions and cults can emerge organically from play, including as consequences of player-NPC interaction. This is an active layer of the world, not background scenery.

## World Textures

- **Coffee economy:** Controlled by five rival noble houses (Gilgane, Westane, Rogarre, Tomsen, Apostol), each holding one irreplaceable link in the chain. The scarcity is manufactured — every bean is sterilized before sale. In-world names: ashwake (growers), the Black (trade). See `data/resources/coffee.md`.
- **Remnant technology:** A pre-history civilization left ruins and relics the present world cannot explain or replicate. Introduce sparingly — one mission or arc element at a time, never as backdrop noise. What it was, what it built, and what ended it are unknown and should stay unknown. Functional relics feel wrong, not evil — they don't behave like magic or craftsmanship. No faction controls or understands this. D&D atmosphere holds; this is just the shadow of something older underneath it.
- **Technological ceiling:** Standard D&D is the common tone. Steampunk Dwemer is the maximum reach — rare, upper bell curve only, associated with extinct or lost civilizations. Never the common infrastructure of a living society. When it appears, it feels inherited rather than built.
- **Optimism persists:** Wonder and warmth exist even in dark settings. Party bonds form through shared experience. Small people from small places are capable of facing a world much larger than them — and knowing it.
- **Wizard guilds:** Organized arcane institutions exist across the world — not dominant, but persistent. They operate more like cartels or professional societies than academies: controlling access to certain magical lineages, enforcing internal codes with extreme prejudice, and treating magic as a commodity with posted prices. Different guilds compete through precedence and client relationships; open conflict is costly and destabilizing, so it's avoided when possible — but guilds do go to war, declare enemies, and field forces when stakes are high enough. Members are recognizable; freelance casters operating outside guild sanction are viewed with suspicion by guild practitioners and clients alike. Inspired by the sorcerous schools of *The Prince of Nothing* (ancient, politically self-interested, philosophically distinct) and the Bondsmagi of *The Lies of Locke Lamora* (neutral-for-hire cartel logic, collective punishment, unknowable internal governance). Background texture — present in major cities and trade hubs, rarely a session's central subject.

## World Tensions

**Tech/faith axis:** Technological advancement and religious devotion exist in tension — but this axis is independent from moral alignment. A highly technological faction can be altruistic, genuinely believing rational progress serves people better than divine dependency. A deeply religious faction can be machiavellian, using faith as a control mechanism. All four quadrants exist and should appear in play.

- Low-tech cultures tend toward devotion; high-tech factions tend toward secularism or open antagonism toward organized religion
- The most technologically advanced factions are the most hostile to faith — not because they are villains, but because their worldview is internally coherent and directly competing
- The Remnant Tech civilization pushed this further than anyone alive has. What happened to them is unknown and should stay unknown. That shadow is load-bearing.

**Captured god:** At least one high-tech faction has captured a god. Which god, its current state, and what the faction is doing with it are DM-controlled and intentionally unspecified — reveal on your terms. The world does not know. Whispers exist — fragments of rumor, unexplained divine silence, cult behavior that doesn't add up. Players may piece it together slowly. Do not establish these details in worldbuilding; establish them in play.

## Influences

### Major
- Lord of the Rings (depth, magic rarity, mythic weight)
- Game of Thrones (political plots, moral complexity)
- Western genre (desert frontier, scarcity, outlaws)
- Pirate genre (sea and sky variants, magical or steampunk ships)
- Elden Ring (shattered ancient orders, cryptic ruins of fallen civilizations, gods who broke the world)
- Kill Bill / Samurai genre (honor codes and their contradictions; the ronin — skilled, masterless, morally complex; craft mastery as identity, not just capability; revenge as a pure and consuming force; stylized violence that is purposeful and ritual, never gratuitous)

### Minor
- Warhammer 40k (planets where history has forgotten mighty ages — technology as lost inheritance, not progress)
- Redwall (tight-knit communities of smallfolk, cozy-dark contrast, simple lives interrupted by genuine menace — not anthro animals, but the texture of beloved places under threat)
- Grandia (optimism as survival — wonder persists despite darkness; party bonds form through shared experience, not exposition; small people from small places facing a world much larger than them)

## What to Avoid

- Pure grimdark without hope — nihilism contradicts the optimism texture; darkness must coexist with warmth
- Comedy-first tone — levity and warmth belong, but the campaign is not a comedy
- Technology beyond the steampunk ceiling — no firearms as common weapons, no industrial infrastructure
- Generic fantasy Europe — every civilization must have distinct cultural character; bland backdrop is a failure state
- Any single flavor dominating every scene — pirates, coffee, dinosaurs, and gothic horror are all spices, not the whole dish
- Gothic horror as persistent default mode — Strahd is a chapter, not the world's identity; don't let it bleed forward
- Tech-forward factions as default antagonists — their position on religion is ideological, not evil; portray with internal coherence and valid motives
